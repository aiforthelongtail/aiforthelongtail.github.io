AI for the Long‑Tail

What generalizes, what works, and when to use it

A proposed workshop at ICLR 2027, California, April 2027

Read the call for papers Email the organizers

The long tail of AI applications

Foundation models promise useful AI for applications that cannot support a large, carefully supervised training effort of their own. Delivering on that promise requires more than success on familiar examples: models must handle the unfamiliar cases, exceptions, and constraints that matter to experts. AI for the Long-Tail (ALT) addresses this challenge across both under-supported domains and specialized tasks within mainstream fields. Its central aim is to connect domain experts facing related learning problems, so that experience and solutions developed in one field can inform another.

Three questions provide the machine learning (ML) basis for this exchange: what generalizes, what works, and when to use it? Participants will compare generalization requirements, effective interventions, and useful human-in-the-loop roles under limited effective supervision, including incomplete annotations, costly expert feedback, and weak outcome signals. ML researchers interested in applications contribute methods and help investigate when an approach can be adapted across settings.

ALT aims to turn scattered application experience into opportunities for method reuse and joint problem-solving. A further outcome is to identify recurring model limitations and application-grounded priorities for foundation-model training and adaptation.

Who should come: domain experts who develop, adapt, or use foundation models, and ML researchers interested in applications, including model training and post-training in academia and industry. Many people are both.

Three questions

What generalizes?

When do model capabilities and gains from adaptation extend beyond the examples or settings in which they were developed?

We invite application-grounded studies of unfamiliar cases, exceptions, changing conditions, and new combinations of requirements. Contributions should clarify what is expected to transfer and test beyond the cases that motivated an improvement.

What works?

Which interventions address which problems, with what supervision and under what conditions?

We welcome practical solutions and informative failures involving adaptation, post-training, retrieval, tools, task decomposition, and expert guidance. The aim is to identify an approach worth adapting, the changes needed, and a test of whether it helps.

When to use it?

Given demonstrated capabilities and remaining uncertainty, what role should a model play, and when are experts, additional tools, or another approach necessary?

We seek studies of task allocation, clarification, expert correction, verification, and deferral, focusing on the outcomes and costs of the whole workflow. Human oversight should be assessed as part of the system, not assumed to guarantee reliability.

Program

A full day, in person. Talks bring findings and cases; two panels turn them into joint problem-solving. The format below is tentative.

  1. OpeningShared questions and the cases collected before the workshop.
  2. Invited talks I: foundation-model perspectivesThree talks on capabilities, generalization, training, and adaptation.
  3. Poster session AAll accepted papers, including tiny and short papers.
  4. Contributed talks A: practical methods and experienceAccepted papers presenting the problem, available supervision, approach, and findings.
  5. Poster session BAuthors may present in either session, or both.
  6. Panel 1: sharing practical experiencePost-training, recursive self-improvement, self-evolving agents, and tool-based workflows: their costs, prerequisites, and suitable uses. Followed by a method-first case exercise.
  7. Invited talks II: meaningful application problemsTwo talks on what experts need, what counts as progress, and where models succeed or fall short.
  8. Contributed talks B: application challenges and failure casesFailures and partial successes from accepted papers.
  9. Panel 2: learning from application failuresDomain experts take part as research collaborators, not task providers. Followed by a problem-first case exercise.
  10. ClosingLessons and next steps for the contributing communities.

How the panels work

Each panel pairs 30 minutes of discussion and Q&A with 30 minutes of interactive case exploration. Before the workshop, speakers, authors, and the wider community can share failures or partial successes. With expert review, selected cases are described in terms of what generalizes, what works, and when to use it. In the room, case contributors, panelists, and the audience refine a candidate intervention and a follow-up test for each case.

With contributors' agreement, we will publish the expert-reviewed cases and lessons on this website and share them with the communities they came from.

Call for papers

The call opens in December 2026. Details below are provisional until the workshop is accepted.

Regular papers
9 pages
Short papers
4 pages
Tiny papers
2 pages

Maximum length, excluding references. Submissions use the ICLR template.

What to submit

Ongoing and original work addressing any of the three questions: methods, empirical studies, evaluation protocols, domain challenges, informative failures, and partial successes. Single-domain studies are welcome. Challenge papers need not propose an algorithm, but should specify the required behavior and show why the difficulty matters in practice. Extensions of published work must identify a substantive addition.

Review

Double-blind, with two to three independent human reviews per paper, combining ML and application expertise. Accepted papers will be public on OpenReview, and tiny and short papers are eligible for contributed talks.

Use of AI

Papers must be primarily human-authored; AI-generated papers will be desk-rejected. Authors may use AI assistance, but must verify its output, disclose substantive use, and remain responsible for the work. AI will not act as an author, reviewer, or decision-maker.

Not writing a paper?

Share an application case instead: a failure or partial success from your field. Case reports need no paper submission or workshop attendance. The call for cases opens alongside the call for papers.
